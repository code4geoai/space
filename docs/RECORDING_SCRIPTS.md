# Narration recording pack

All six stories in all three languages are fictional first-person narration based on cited NASA mission facts. Each machine is a storytelling character, not a speaking historical witness. The Urdu stories have been reviewed by the project owner. The Pashto is an **unreviewed draft**: a fluent speaker should check meaning, pronunciation and suitability before recording or public release. Do not claim NASA approval.

No student has supplied a recording yet. Do not create synthetic voices and label them student recordings. Obtain the student’s and guardian’s permission before publishing a minor’s voice. Keep permission records privately, outside this repository; public credit can be a chosen nickname or omitted entirely.

## English

### Apollo 11 descent stage → audio/en/apollo11.mp3

1. **The descent.** I am the descent stage of Apollo 11, and I helped carry two explorers to the Moon. They were Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin. On July 20, 1969, my engine slowed us down above the Moon’s surface, over the Sea of Tranquility. Neil saw big boulders below, so he took control himself and flew on to find a safe place. When we touched down, we had only seconds of fuel left.
2. **On the surface.** Look closely at my feet. They sank only a little into the Moon’s dust, and they held us steady. Neil climbed down the ladder on my leg, and Buzz came after him. Those were the first human footprints on another world.
3. **Left behind.** My upper stage took them back toward home; my gold-covered lower stage stayed behind on the Moon. That day I was their launch pad, and I have not moved since. You can imagine me as a quiet marker of that first landing.

### Apollo 17 lunar rover → audio/en/rover.mp3

1. **Through the valley.** I am the Apollo 17 lunar rover, and my wheels carried astronauts and their tools across a dusty valley. In December 1972, Gene Cernan and Harrison Schmitt drove me through the Taurus-Littrow valley. I ran on batteries, and my wheels were made of wire mesh. I could help them explore farther than walking alone. Together we traveled more than 35 kilometers.
2. **Keeping in touch.** I carried an antenna and a television camera, so people on Earth could follow the journey. Harrison was a geologist, and at Shorty Crater he found orange soil. It held volcanic glass about 3.5 billion years old.
3. **A repair on the Moon.** Then my fender broke off, and dust began to spray over the astronauts. They made a new fender from four maps, tape and clamps, and it worked. I am still parked in that valley. My tracks tell a story of people learning to travel on another world.

### Lunar laser retroreflector → audio/en/reflector.mp3

1. **One hundred small prisms.** I am a panel of one hundred small glass prisms. Send me a little light, and I send it back, straight to where it came from. Scientists aim a laser at me from Earth, and the light returns in about two and a half seconds.
2. **Carried by hand.** In July 1969, Buzz Aldrin carried me across the Moon in one hand. He and Neil Armstrong set me on the dusty ground and turned me to face Earth. I do not need wheels or a voice. I do not need electricity either.
3. **A quiet reply.** In this imagined conversation, my quiet reply helps people measure the space between Earth and the Moon. It showed that the Moon moves about 3.8 centimeters farther from Earth every year. More than fifty years later, I am still answering.

### Opportunity rover → audio/en/opportunity.mp3

1. **Built for 90 days.** I landed on Mars in January 2004, inside a small crater called Eagle. My twin, Spirit, had landed on the other side of Mars about 20 days before me. I was built to work for just 90 Martian days. But I ran on sunlight, the wind sometimes cleaned the dust off my solar panels, and I kept going for almost 15 years.
2. **Reading the rocks.** I rolled across Mars, reading rocks like pages in an old book. They held clues to water long ago, and to a time when Mars may have been able to support tiny living things. I drove 45 kilometers, farther than a marathon, and my shadow came with me all the way.
3. **The last storm.** In June 2018, a huge dust storm covered Mars and hid the Sun. My signal went silent. NASA called to me for months, and ended my mission in February 2019. My wheels are still now, but the questions I helped people ask keep moving.

### Ingenuity helicopter → audio/en/ingenuity.mp3

1. **A small beginning.** I am Ingenuity. I arrived on Mars with Perseverance, a small machine carrying a big question: could I fly here?
2. **The first leap.** On April 19, 2021, I flew up above the surface of Mars. My camera caught my shadow: a little shape with a new possibility.
3. **A new perspective.** From the air, I could see the ground differently. Just imagine the excitement of looking at a familiar world in a completely new way. The surface air on Mars is about 1% as dense as Earth’s air at sea level, so making enough lift is difficult.
4. **Traces of a journey.** Later, I photographed the parachute and backshell, the protective cover, that helped Perseverance land. Even the leftover equipment here can tell an engineering story.
5. **A legacy beyond flight.** I completed seventy-two flights. My final flight damaged my rotors. I cannot fly now, but what we learned will help the people who come next.

### Perseverance landing debris → audio/en/debris.mp3

1. **A piece of the journey.** I am part of the space journey too. I am not the explorer myself, but my parachute helped slow a rover on its way down. I am the backshell, the protective shell and cover around the rover. In February 2021, the two of us fell through the thin air of Mars together.
2. **A hidden message.** My parachute opened 21.5 meters wide, and its pattern hid a secret message: Dare Mighty Things. When we had slowed down enough, I let go, and the rover flew on with its rockets.
3. **Still useful.** Left on the ground, I can still help engineers learn how a landing worked. In April 2022, Ingenuity flew over me and took my picture. My parachute showed no damage from that fast ride. Those photos may help future spacecraft land more safely.

## Record, review, install

1. Review `data/content.js` with a fluent speaker and a teacher. Record the story sections only, in order; do not read headings unless you also include their duration in cues. Leave natural pauses. Use a quiet room and a comfortable pace. Timing is measured after recording, rather than forcing a child to match a timer.
2. The local recorder requires HTTPS or localhost and microphone permission. It downloads WebM (or Ogg if the browser provides it). It does not upload. Convert to actual MP3 in a trusted audio editor; renaming a WebM file is insufficient. An external recorder is equally suitable.
3. Export mono MP3 at 96–128 kbps. Trim long silence and avoid clipping. Listen to the complete result with the narrator and guardian. Save at the exact path above; six artifact IDs are `apollo11`, `rover`, `reflector`, `opportunity`, `ingenuity`, `debris`. Other supported language folders are `ar`, `fr`, `es`.
4. In `data/media.js`, add a recording entry (the following is a **schema example, not a claim that an MP3 exists**):

```js
"ur/ingenuity": {
  available: true,
  file: "audio/ur/ingenuity.mp3",
  duration: 68.4,
  cues: [0, 13.2, 25.6, 38.7, 52.1]
}
```

5. Replace the example numbers with the actual start of each spoken segment and exact duration. Five increasing cue starts, beginning at zero, are required for Ingenuity. Each other artifact has three story sections, one per photograph, and needs three increasing cue starts, beginning at zero. Measure the start of each section. Captions show one sentence at a time; within a section they change in proportion to sentence length, so a recording's captions are an estimate between cues. The player uses MP3 `currentTime`, not a second independent timer, so pause, seek and playback speed keep scenes/subtitles together.
6. Run `npm run check`, `node scripts/cache.mjs`, `npm test`, and `npm run build`. A recording marked available but missing from disk fails checks. Rebuild and redeploy to include it in the offline cache.
7. For a source change, keep this script pack and the scene tables in `data/content.js` identical. Changing language/artifact stops playback; the application never substitutes an unrelated language voice. The default listening mode prefers the reviewed student MP3 and otherwise uses a matching device voice after the visitor presses Play. Device narration follows sentence and scene completion; the silent mode remains available. A device voice is never labeled as a student recording.

The silent demonstration uses five 12-second scenes (60 seconds) for Ingenuity and three 20-second scenes (60 seconds) for each other story; these are presentation timings, not final recording cues. A recording can have different timings for every language.
